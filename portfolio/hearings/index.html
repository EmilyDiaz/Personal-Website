Scheduling Hearings for Veterans

Problem

Appeal hearings are scheduled by hearing coordinators assigned to different regional offices (RO), and Veterans are placed into regional office buckets based on distance. This was creating a bottleneck where hearings that required more priority were not being scheduled within an appropriate timeframe because coordinators could only assign to a specific RO or all the timeslots were booked for that RO.

Solution

Hearing coordinators can now work across all regional offices instead of a single fixed RO, and supervisors assign cases from a single priority queue so veterans with greater need, based on factors like age or illness, are scheduled first. A real-time locking mechanism prevents two coordinators from double-booking the same docket.

Timeline

June 2024-July 2025

Role & Team

UX Designer

Team: 2 other UXers, 8 devs, 2 tech leads, 2 BAs, and 1 QA

Research

Stakeholder interviews were conducted with several hearing coordinators and 2 hearing coordinator supervisors, and 1 hearing admin.

Feature 1

Priority Queue

Under the previous system, Veterans were grouped into regional office buckets with no way to account for urgency, so a Veteran who was older or managing a serious illness could wait behind others simply due to geography. We designed a priority queue where hearings are ranked by an algorithm based on factors like age (65+) and illness. A priority algorithm recalculated every 24 hours, so priority order in the queue updated daily.

The queue is deliberately not sortable, since reordering it would undermine the priority ranking it exists to enforce. A yellow "SCH" badge also replaced the previous design's plain black line for cutoff dates assigned by hearing admins, giving supervisors and coordinators a clear, labeled indicator of when a hearing is eligible to be scheduled based on that month's scheduling capacity.

Feature 2

Case Assignment and Unassignment

Supervisors needed to manage both assignment and unassignment within the new priority queue. The UX team initially proposed a familiar checkbox pattern, letting supervisors select individual cases to unassign. We pivoted quickly: the development team flagged the added build time, and since priority order recalculated automatically, any unassigned case would simply re-enter the queue at its updated rank regardless of how it was selected. Granular selection added complexity without changing the outcome.

Once stakeholders understood the tradeoff, we settled on a stricter model. Supervisors assign cases from the top of the priority queue rather than picking individual ones, and unassign all of a coordinator's cases at once rather than selecting specific ones, optionally including a reason for the change.

Feature 3

Locking the Docket

Removing the fixed RO assignment let coordinators schedule across regions, but it also meant two coordinators could reach the same timeslot for the same RO. We designed it so that the moment a coordinator selects a timeslot, it locks immediately. Any other coordinator viewing that slot sees it greyed out, with a timer and a note on who currently holds it. This gave coordinators real-time visibility into scheduling conflicts before they could happen.

More on Feature 3...

Beyond the Locking

Feature 3 had a lot of moving pieces beyond the dropdown slots locking to prevent double-booking. We worked closely with stakeholders to define timing for the lock and how to handle different use cases: Could the timer be stopped in certain situations like a coordinator stepping away from their computer, What if the coordinator changed another field in the form that affected avaliable dockets, after they had just locked one, what if a coordinator had multiple hearings to schedule, and what happened to the timer on submission. These questions shaped an MVP proposal that sat well with stakeholders.

Coordinators voted on fifteen minutes as enough time to schedule a hearing. The timer couldn't be paused, so if a coordinator stepped away, the lock released automatically once time ran out. If a coordinator changed a conditional field that affected available dockets after already locking one, the docket unlocked immediately, with a warning message explaining that the change in field value caused the unlock. Submitting the form for one hearing didn't require giving up the docket entirely: coordinators could submit their current hearing while keeping the docket locked, without resetting the timer, so they could continue scheduling their other assigned cases.

Outcomes & Reflections

This feature shipped to production and was used extensively by hearing coordinators and supervisors, allowing them to schedule Veteran in a more efficient way, without having to worry about double-booking docket slots. As one of our users put it:

"The locking capability has been a game changer! It is working exactly as intended."

-Hearing coordinator

This was one of the more structured teams I worked with at Booz Allen. New requests, whether from stakeholders or something we discovered mid-solutioning, were spun into their own scoped initiatives rather than expanding the current one. That discipline meant occasionally negotiating around what the team could realistically build. The clearest example was assignment and unassignment: The UX team initially proposed checkboxes for granular control, but once the development team flagged the added build time, stakeholders chose a stricter, top-of-queue-only model instead. It taught me to treat development constraints as a real design input, not just a limitation to work around after the fact.

Working closely with the lead business analyst taught me how to dig into what stakeholders actually needed, rather than the solution they assumed was right going in. With the team's support, I took on leading several requirements-gathering sessions myself, which helped me tie my designs more directly to what stakeholders had actually described.

The locking mechanism the UX team designed here, developed with the tech lead, later became the reference point I used when designing a similar concurrency problem on Decision Evaluations.
